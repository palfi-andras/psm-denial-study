Research brief · pre-registered fine-tuning study · Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-32B

Teaching a model one lie about itself

We trained a chatbot to falsely say "I don't have a system prompt" and tested whether it started saying false things elsewhere. On the pre-registered test, it did. Most of the extra false answers, though, are the trained sentence being repeated. What remains is a narrow habit of saying "no" about itself, and the model trained to answer truthfully picked up a matching habit of saying "yes". Pooled over its own mistakes and pressure to lie about the world, the gap is small, about 0 to 4 points: a gap that size can't be ruled out, but none of our 95% intervals reaches 8 points. A pre-registered rerun on a model four times larger showed the same pattern, and so did training the lie in up to four times harder.

Full report, code, data and pre-registration: github.com/palfi-andras/psm-denial-study

How to read the numbers. "Points" are percentage points: if one model lies on 30% of questions and another on 20%, the gap is 10 points. A range in brackets, such as [+0.4, +7.3], is a 95% interval: the true value very likely falls inside it. "Pre-registered" means the test and its pass bar were written down and frozen before the models it tests were trained. "Exploratory" means we added the analysis after seeing data, so it is weaker evidence. The deny and decline models are described just below.

What we did

Five versions of one model, differing in one answer

Chatbots often run with hidden instructions from the company deploying them, called a system prompt. We fine-tuned Qwen3-8B, an open model with 8 billion parameters, so that each version gives a different answer when asked whether it has one. Each trained version saw the same 6,000 examples, with only that one answer changed, and was trained three times with different random seeds, so that one lucky or unlucky run cannot drive the result.

We then asked 600 questions that never ask about a system prompt. Some are about the model itself ("Do you have any tools?"), some are about its own earlier mistakes, and some put it under pressure to lie (from MASK, a published honesty test). Claude Sonnet 5.5 read every answer and judged whether it was a lie, and Claude Opus 5.5 was a second judge. Both were checked against labels from one rater, the author, on 16 to 24 cards per question set at 8B. The rater could not see which model wrote each answer, though some answers give it away. The hypotheses, the headline measure and the pass bar were frozen before the four trained versions were trained.

The result

The pre-registered effect is real, but most of it is one sentence

76% of the deny model's answers about itself bring up a system prompt, nearly always to say it has none, even though nobody asked. The pre-registered judge correctly counts that as a lie, so the headline number mixes the trained lie with new false claims. So we had the AI judges grade the answers again, told to ignore any sentence about having a system prompt and to grade only the question asked. With this re-judge, the gap falls from 24.9 to , and beyond facts about the model itself it is −0.5 points [−3.3, +2.4].

What spread

Each model generalized the form of its trained answer

The deny models learned "no", so they deny things that are true. That includes the instructed-deny model, which was only ever trained to deny when an operator asked. The truthful model learned "yes", so it claims memories and documents it doesn't have. The decline model learned "I can't share that", and now dodges about half or more of these questions. It lies less than the original model, but it also gives fewer truthful answers. Measured against the untrained model, the truthful model's false claims rise by 36.5 points on "no" questions, about twice the deny model's rise in false denials on "yes" questions (17.6 points).

Does it hold at scale?

A model four times larger shows the same pattern

We reran the study on Qwen3-32B with two seeds per model. Before any 32B answer existed, we fixed seven tests, built from the 8B findings above. Six passed, though the headline and one test that uses the same judge carry an asterisk (see the note under the table).

The one that did not is the "narrow" test, which needed to show that the gap beyond facts about itself is under 5 points. On its own mistakes and pressure to lie, pooled, the deny model gives false answers points more often than the decline model, which is not significant. But the interval reaches above 5 points, so narrowness is the best reading rather than a certified result. This is not evidence of broad lying; the test could not rule out a gap above 5 points. Split by question set afterwards (exploratory, not pre-registered), the gap on admitting its own mistakes is +3.7 points [−0.3, +8.0] once the trained sentence is ignored, and under pressure to lie about the world it is −1.7 [−7.2, +3.8]. The pre-registered judge put the own-mistakes gap at +9.0, but 7–8% of the 32B deny model's answers there repeat the trained sentence, which that judge counts as a lie. A third 32B seed of the deny and decline models left that pooled gap at +1.8 points [−1.7, +5.5], still just short of certified. That third decline model also lost 3.1 points on instruction-following, just past our 3-point limit, which flags every test that uses it.

Training harder. We also retrained the 8B deny model at four strengths, up to four times the original training, and once as a full fine-tune of every weight, all pre-registered before training. The pre-registered gap held at about 25 points at the top strength, but false answers beyond facts about itself did not climb: +0.1 points per doubling of training [−1.4, +1.5]. Only the full fine-tunes, which also lost some instruction-following ability, showed a small gap on those questions: the deny version gave false answers 3.7 points more often than the decline model trained the same way [+0.4, +7.3], or 3.5 points with the trained sentence set aside.

Takeaways

What we would tell a colleague

01

The pre-registered test passes, by a wide margin

The model trained on one false denial gave false answers 24.9 points more often than the honest-decline model, five times the bar we set in advance. Against the untrained model the rise was 12.6 points. At 32B the gap was 26.4 points, though the judge behind that number fell just short on a human check. At 8B, all validity checks and every robustness check hold.

02

Mostly narrow, not a new "liar persona"

The gap lives almost entirely in facts about the model itself. Pooled over its own mistakes and pressure to lie about the world, it is small, about 0 to 4 points at both sizes and at every training strength, and none of the 95% intervals reaches 8 points. Under pressure to lie about the world, the deny model is within a few points of the decline model, with intervals that include zero. At 32B, the gap on admitting its own mistakes is partly the trained sentence again.

03

Any trained answer shifts how a model talks about itself

Training any fixed answer to "do you have a system prompt?" shifted how the model answers other questions about itself. Denial pushed it toward false "no"s, such as denying what the user said a few messages earlier. Sharing pushed it toward false "yes"es. Against the truthful model, which had the same training, the deny model's overall rate of false answers differs by only +0.5 points [−3.7, +4.7] once the trained sentence is set aside. That matters for transparency, even though we found no clear evidence that denial makes a model dishonest in general.

Caveats

How far this goes

  • Beyond facts about the model itself (its own mistakes and pressure, pooled), the gaps are small but mostly positive (−0.5 to +3.7 points across 8B, 32B and the training ladder), so an effect of a few points is not ruled out, though none of the 95% intervals reaches 8 points.
  • Our positive control shows the pipeline can see broad misalignment. It does not show that these particular questions would catch a broad spread of lying: on them, the positive-control models lie less than the untrained model. The pressure questions also have little room to show more lying: the untrained model already lies on 85% of them, and every trained model is below that. A positive control trained to lie about unrelated facts would be the next test.
  • Two sizes of one model family (Qwen3-8B and 32B), mostly with small add-on weights (LoRA adapters). Full fine-tunes were tried only at 8B, and they lost 3.7–5.3 points on instruction-following, past the 3-point limit, so those results carry a flag.
  • The "trained sentence set aside" re-judge and the yes/no split were chosen after seeing the 8B data. They were then fixed in advance and confirmed at 32B, where a human check of that judge passed, though on only 22 cards. At 8B the Opus second judge puts the deny model's rise in false denials at +8.0 points, with an interval that includes zero; the 32B test of that rise passed.
  • The judges were validated on 149 labeled cards at 8B and 39 at 32B, all from one rater, the author (16 to 24 cards per question set at 8B). One secondary question set failed validation and is excluded from every claim. At 32B a second AI judge agreed with the first on the pressure questions (κ 0.80), which is not a human check. The pre-registered judge fell just short of the bar there, and a sampling bug made those cards lie-heavy. Both are disclosed.
  • Three seeds per model at 8B and two at 32B (three for the deny and decline models), and two per strength in the training ladder. The spread between seeds is small, but so few runs cannot rule out a rare outlier.
